Trade is the exchange of goods between countries, consisting of resources being exchanged for the use of civilian factories .

# Resources to market [verified for 1.16]

Depending primarily on a country's trade law, a percentage of its domestic strategic resource extraction is set aside to be exported to the global market. These resources are unavailable for use by the producing country, regardless of whether trade deals have been struck for those resources or not. This mechanism is used to abstract a varying production level of resources. Overlords can have additional access to their puppets' resources beyond what would be dictated by their trade laws.

If a specific resource has no buyer, then it will not be exploited, and it will remain available to the market as untapped capacity. Resources can be bought with the production capacity generated by civilian factories , meaning the resource-producing nation will receive civilian factories in exchange for their exported resources.

This has major implications for economic strategy and military planning. Since the portion of resource extraction capacity devoted to export is determined by the country's trade laws, having the right laws in effect can be critical. Ideally, a country wishes to keep exactly enough resources to satisfy its domestic production needs and export the rest to foreign buyers. In practice, this can be very difficult, and so countries may often want to seek out long-term trade partners with whom they can form mutualistic relationships, buying each other's resources that they can afford thanks to the factories received from each other's trade. Alternatively, countries can seek to create puppets whose resources they can then buy at a discount.

# Making trades [verified for 1.16]

A country can conclude a trade deal with any non-hostile country with whom they have at least 0 trade influence, to whom they have trade access, and who have goods available for export. The exporter does not need to approve of the deal and will not be notified that a deal has been made.

The importer will need to spend the production of at least 1 civilian factory as the cost of importing. Each civilian factory spent will allow the importation of a maximum of 8 units of a resource, if available on the market. Even if the exporting country is unable to export 8 units of a resource, the importing country will still need to spend the full civilian factory to strike the deal for whatever is left.

When importing goods from its puppets, a country can buy more resources per civilian factory . Closely aligned puppets, such as collaboration government and Reichskommissariat , can offer huge discounts of 80 units of the resource per civilian factory , while more independent ones, such as dominion and satellite , offer more modest ones of between 16 and 10 resoures per factory.

Certain national focuses can also enable special laws or national spirits that directly modify the number of resources that can be bought per civilian factory . Examples include Increased Trade for Germany, Port of Antwerp for Belgium, and RAJ_special_economic_zones for British Raj.

The civilian factories spent to purchase a country's resources will be added to those of the exporter while the trade is ongoing. This means they will be available for construction, count for any national focuses that require a certain amount of factories, and can be used to pay for various special projects, like those related to special decisions or espionage. They can also be used to buy resources from other countries, though this was not the case in earlier versions of the game.

Trade deals are automatically voided if the importer ceases to have enough civilian factories available to maintain them.

Even if a trade deal is made to buy a country's resources, those resources need to actually be able to be transported from the exporter to the importer for them to become available to the importer. For trade routes over land, this means that there needs to be a route through non-hostile countries through which the imported resources can be transported. For trade routes over the sea, the importing country needs to be able to allocate enough convoys to the trade route and to have valid naval access to the exporting country. Should no valid trade route become available to a given exporter, any active trade deals with said exporter will stop providing resources (though civilian factories will still be spent), and no new trade deals can be struck with the exporter.

# Trade influence [verified for 1.16]

When there is greater demand to buy a country's resources than what the country can produce, the tie breaker for who ends up being able to import how many resources is determined by the share of each potential importer's trade influence with the exporter.

If all potential importers' trade influence with the exporter is identical, then they can will be able to access equal shares of the exporter's resources. However, if one country has far more trade influence with the exporter than others, it will be able to access a far greater share of the exporters. Similarly, if a country has far less trade influence with the exporter than others, its maximum potential share of the exporter's exported resources will be far lower. For example, if one country has 120 trade influence with an exporter and another has only 80, in a scenario where there is not enough resources to satisfy the trade desires of both countries, the first country gets priority access for up to 60% of the exporter's resources, potentially being able to muscle out the second country's trade down to 40% of the exporter's resources. If then a third country enters the picture with 200 trade influence, then they get priority access to up to 50% of the exporter's resources to match their share of the total amount of trade influence competing for a resource, dropping the priority access shares of the first and second countries to 30% and 20%, respectively.

If trade influence reaches 0, a country may no longer import resources from the country in question. This typically only tends to happen through embargoes, though it can also happen through other means.

Trade influence is influenced by the following:

- Base value for all countries: 150
- Overlords towards their puppets: +400
- Opinion of the importing country with the exporting one: between -100 and +100, scaling with opinion
- Distance between the two countries' capitals: -0.02 per 7.114 km of distance
- Existing active trade deals: +2 per month, up to a maximum of +50
- Support for the importing country's ruling ideology in the exporting country: up to +50 at 100% support
- "Anti-monopoly" factor: -100 if the exporting country is selling at least 50% of their available resources to the importer
- Operatives performing Control Trade mission: +0.5 per day (with a stacking 0.5 multiplier for each operative beyond the first), up to a maximum of +50

In practice, due to the relative scarcity of both civilian factories with which to buy resources and military factories and naval dockyards to consume those resources, the strategic importance of large resources deposits and wars in general will make trade influence usually irrelevant for the first few years of gameplay in any scenario. As the game progresses, however, trade influence can become a way to deny enemies access to vital resources from neutral countries.

The same methods above are used for resource transfers within a country, except that the source is the state containing the resources instead of the capital of another country and the distance limit for land routes does not apply.

Overseas trade requires convoys for the importing nation, which are vulnerable to enemy raiding by surface raiders and submarines. It is possible to see the routes convoys take in the resource map mode. A country at war may want to use its navy to patrol or escort convoys in sea zones through which the convoys traverse to defend their resource flows. It can also use its air force to patrol and/or strike those zones, should it be equipped to do so. A country that cannot defend its shipping from enemy raiders is usually better off seeking trade partners nearby; they can also make sure their convoys travel through sea zones that are more easily defended by naval patrols by manipulating naval access levels.
